FlexGaussian: Flexible and Cost-Effective Training-Free Compression for 3D Gaussian Splatting

ACMMM2025

Boyuan Tian, Qizhe Gao, Siran Xianyu, Xiaotong Cui, Minjia Zhang

University of illinois at Urbana Champaign

📄 Paper ✖ arXiv 💻 Code

Intuition

3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) is a new and powerful technique that allow users to view complex and photorealistic 3D scenes in real-time. But it does comes with a price: what actually represents the static scenes are large models that can be unwieldy to store and transfer. When running the algorithm on edge devices with limited storage, the large model size can easily eat up space.

With the idea that reducing 3DGS model size bring us the flexibility of extending its usage to various platforms, we focused on ways to produce a leaner model without ruining the original scene.

Methodology

➤ How to make the model lightweight

After delving into the storage structure of the original 3DGS design, we found out why it takes us hundreds of megabytes to store a scene of a garden. The scene is essentially a point cloud that comprises hundreds of thousands of colored ellipsoids named Gaussian points. Before they are rendered into what we see in real time, each Gaussian point is stored as a vector containing the ellipsoid center’s 3D coordinate, scaling factor, rotation quaternion, base color, and spherical harmonic (SH) coefficients. Especially, when SH degree is chosen to be 3 for higher reconstruction quality, 3(l + 1) ^2 = 48 parameters are needed to store the color coefficients of one gaussian. When all parameters are stored as 32-bit floating-point numbers, and we need to store all points in the point cloud no matter their importance, storing the model will be expensive.

➤Key design ideas

Given the point-based nature of the models, we approached the compression problem of 3DGS models by identifying points and attributes that contribute less to the rendering results. Furthermore, we use a more compact structure with fewer bits used to store the "less important" attributes. The two stages of our method were developed independently and later integrated into one pipeline that gives us the final compressed model.

➤Attribute-Discriminative Pruning

We begin the pipeline by identifying points with less contribution to what is displayed on our screen with the help of the importance score introduced in LightGaussian. This metric considers the Gaussian point's contribution to global pixels, opacity, and volumetric density. With a one-time calculation of the importance scores of the Gaussians, we can perform pruning by retaining the top α% of Gaussians and discarding the bottom β% that contribute minimally to quality. Since no re-training and recalculating of importance score are involved in this efficient process, we have time to visit several pruning parameter configurations to determine which one is optimal in the limited set of parameter pairs. This helps us find a pruning setting unique to the scene we want to compress, while considering both quality and the compression ratio.

➤Precision-Aware Bit-Width Quantization

One major contributor to the large file size of 3D Gaussian models is storing all coefficients as 32-bit floating point values. To address this, we apply quantization to reduce bit-widths while preserving rendering quality. Specifically, we quantize parameters after training and dequantize them during loading, before feeding them into the rendering pipeline. The sensitivity of each parameter group is measured by the PSNR drop between images rendered from the original and quantized models.

In our implementation, we choose 4-bit, 8-bit, and 16-bit quantization levels. These powers-of-two bit widths ensure memory alignment and enable efficient bit-packing, simplifying compression and decompression logic. While finer-grained precision might offer more flexibility, it introduces complex bit manipulation and often degrades runtime efficiency.

The trade-offs across precision levels are as follows:

  • 16-bit: Maintains high visual fidelity but yields a modest compression ratio.
  • 8-bit: Strikes a balance between storage savings and acceptable visual loss.
  • 4-bit: Provides the highest compression but introduces noticeable degradation.

While some attributes can be aggressively quantized, we advise against applying low-bit quantization to the ellipsoid center positions. For example, 4-bit quantization limits each of the x, y, and z axes to just 16 discrete values—effectively turning the continuous 3D space into a coarse voxel grid and significantly distorting the scene layout.

➤Overview of Flexgaussian

FlexGaussian first applies attribute-discriminative pruning (ADP) to obtain semi-structurally pruned Gaussian primitives. It then employs INT8/INT4 channel-wise mixed-precision quantization (MPQ) to further reduce the model size along the bit dimension. Finally, FlexGaussian introduces a novel lightweight online adaptation (FOA) algorithm to adaptively adjust the compression ratio for diverse scenes on hardware with different efficiency constraints.

Evaluation

We evaluate FlexGaussian on Mip-NeRF360, Tanks&Temples, and Deep Blending—spanning diverse indoor and outdoor scenes. Compared to the original 3DGS and several compression baselines (e.g., FCGS, LightGaussian, CompGS), FlexGaussian achieves up to 96.4% compression with <1 dB PSNR drop. It runs 1.7×–2.1× faster than FCGS and up to 100× faster than retraining-based methods. All experiments were run on an RTX 3090, with additional tests on Jetson Xavier confirming efficiency on edge devices.
